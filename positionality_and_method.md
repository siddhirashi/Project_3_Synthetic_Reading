# Positionality and method (step 2)

Written after reading [`partial_reading.txt`](partial_reading.txt) (28 of 140 paragraphs, 7 from each quarter of the story) and before running the model.

## What I noticed in the partial reading

- **Inside and outside are swapped, and the narrator is the one who is surprised.** She asks "Where are the men?" (1.29) and is told they are kept in their "proper places". She answers with the line that gives the idea away: "Just as we are kept in the zenana?" (1.33).
- **The argument is made as a complaint about fairness.** Sister Sara says "how unfair it is to shut in the harmless women and let loose the men" (2.1), and the narrator can only reply by asking whether men's business, political and commercial, could ever be moved indoors (2.16).
- **Knowledge belongs to the women.** In the sampled lines the women collect sun-heat and draw water from the air, while the men build up military power and laugh at the universities as "a sentimental nightmare" (3.8). The narrator admits that her own "scientific knowledge was very limited" (3.7).
- **Purdah is used as a tool.** In part 3 the Lady Principal asks that "the men must enter the zenanas" and says she makes the request "for the sake of purdah" (3.29).
- **What puzzled me.** The story sounds fully confident in its argument, but it is presented as a dream, and the narrator is not sure whether she was awake. I wanted to know whether the dream frame protects the argument or weakens it.

## Research problem

How does *Sultana's Dream* reverse the gendered division of space, work and knowledge, and what does that reversal say about the purdah system it is criticising?

## Frameworks I will use and why

**Main: feminist standpoint theory** (Sandra Harding; Nancy Hartsock). It asks whose position knowledge is produced from. That fits the sampled lines directly, where women make science and men dismiss it. What it cannot see by itself is the colonial setting: the story is written in English, for an English-language magazine in Madras, in British India.

**Supporting: feminist spatial criticism** (Doreen Massey). It treats space as something produced by social relations, not as a neutral container. That fits the zenana and mardana, and the idea of people being kept in their "proper places". What it cannot do is say much about the story's science and technology.

## How the LLM comes into it

I ask the model to find passages in each quarter of the story that bear on eight themes, and to give a word-for-word quotation for each. That is a fair job for a model, because it is a matter of finding and sorting. It is not asked to decide what a passage means. I check every quotation against the text by script, and read the unverified ones by hand. The interpretation, in [`interpretation.md`](interpretation.md), is mine.
